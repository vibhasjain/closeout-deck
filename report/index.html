The two industries fail differently

Healthcare workers are more reliable about showing up at all, but once en route, they run later. Light industrial is the mirror image: weaker show rates, stronger punctuality among those who come. A credible target has to be set per industry, not across the board.

Why shifts start late21.9%

Lateness begins at home

Late departure is the biggest reason: roughly one in two late arrivals (45%) simply left too late. The commute takes another 40%: long stops on route (17%), traffic (12%), detours (11%). A last-minute assignment, the shift assigned with less time left than the commute needs, adds another 11%.

The industries diverge: healthcare is a departure-and-stop story (late departure 47%, long stop 17%, traffic just 8%), light industrial spreads onto the commute (late departure 41%, traffic 19%, long stop 16%). A last-minute assignment, assigned with less time left than the commute, bites harder in healthcare (13%) than light industrial (7%). Other reasons (~5%) bundles the two smallest: reached the vicinity on time and no single clear reason.

Lateness leaves home with the worker: half of late arrivals were already late before the commute began.

Where the metric moves

Same metric, different cuts

The headline number hides its most useful lessons. On-time arrival changes meaningfully and predictably with the clock and the map. These are the two cuts every operator should know about their own book of business.

The clock: mornings are the problem, nights are not

Intuition blames the nights; the data blames the mornings. Healthcare mornings are the weakest cell in the market: one in three arrivals is late, and 52% of that lateness starts at home. Mornings are lost before departure, not on the commute.

By state: a wide gap

Within each industry, the best and toughest states sit 13–15 points apart on identical execution: healthcare states run 64.2–79.6% on time, light industrial 69.3–82.7%. The rate is a geography problem; the reason is the same late-departure problem almost everywhere. The exception is NY, where the commute, not the departure, takes most of the lateness.

What we can't verify3.9%

Workers switching off visibility

For a small share of workers we know they arrived — but not when: they went invisible on the way in. Nine times out of ten it was the worker — the app killed (87% / 77%) or location permissions switched off (8% / 15%). Technical causes are under a tenth — and even faked GPS doesn't slip through: every mocked-location arrival is detected and flagged.

The playbook

Four actions that move the needle

departs late1 of 2
Watch departures

Nudge the worker to leave on time — half of workers who arrive late left home late. Mornings need it most (one in three healthcare morning arrivals is late), so confirm the evening before.

late on the commute3 of 10
Plan the commute before the shift

Budget for stops and traffic when the shift is assigned — the commute drives three in ten lates; in light industrial, traffic alone is nearly one in five.

switches off9 of 10
Hold workers accountable for visibility

Make staying visible a term of the shift and count it in the worker's reliability score — nine out of ten unverifiable shifts trace back to the worker switching off their own visibility.

state spread16 pts
One target doesn't fit fifty states

Set targets against the local baseline and grade movement, not the raw number — the best states run ~80% on time, the toughest 65%, on identical execution.
