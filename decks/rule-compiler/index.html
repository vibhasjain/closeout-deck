The rule compiler

How Closeout Copilot turns plain-English payroll rules into checks a customer can trust with money — what we built, why it exists, and how every piece works.

Open the live workbench ↗ 15 slides · ~12 minutes · N for notes

Framing: this is a teaching deck, not a pitch. By the end everyone should be able to explain four words — rulebook, vocabulary, compiler, engine — and click through the live prototype themselves.

Why this exists

Payroll ops is detective work, and it runs on rules.

Every shift produces two competing time records — the client's clock and ours — plus GPS, badges, schedules and messages. A payroll operator holds each shift against everything they know, spots what breaks a pattern, chases the human who knows, and only then pays and invoices.

The rules already exist

"Zero hours on an active day. Thirty-plus hours of overtime. A missed punch." Operators run these checks by hand today — 15 to 30 minutes per disputed shift, across spreadsheets.

The problem we set out to solve

Customers can state their rules in plain English. How do we run those rules on money — without asking anyone to trust a chatbot?

That sentence is the whole project. This message has to become something that behaves identically every single time, can be tested before it touches a paycheck, and can show an auditor exactly why it fired:

Sam T. · Payroll ops · Slack, Mon 9:12am"No overtime at Mercy General this week please."

Pause here. Everyone agrees LLMs can read rules. The hard part is that payroll is a domain where a wrong answer is a wage claim — "usually right" is not a product.

What we ruled out — 1 of 2

Throw the rulebook into the LLM's context window.

Write every rule in a document, hand it to the model with each shift, hope. This is what "just use AI" means by default — and customers are right to refuse it.

  • Different answer on different runs. The same shift can pass on Tuesday and flag on Wednesday.
  • Untestable. There is no way to know what a rule change does before it touches real payouts.
  • Unauditable. No trace of which rule fired, or why. "The model decided" is not an answer a wage claim accepts.
  • The evidence is damning even at the high end. Stanford found the professional legal-research AIs — Westlaw's and Lexis's own tools — hallucinate on 17–33% of queries.

The Stanford RegLab study is the killer citation: purpose-built, retrieval-grounded, professionally-priced legal AI still fabricates. If that's the ceiling for "read the rules and answer," it cannot be the mechanism that computes pay.

What we ruled out — 2 of 2

Four hundred settings screens.

The incumbent answer is fully deterministic — and it's why implementations take forever. UKG's pay rules are assembled from "work rule building blocks" by consultants: $300K–$750K and 3–18 months per deployment, with a whole consulting industry living off the configuration.

  • Deterministic, but dead. The rules end up divorced from their sources, unreadable by the operator, and changeable only by the people who installed them.
  • Nothing ships pre-built in the US. UKG, Workday and Dayforce ship empty engines. Nobody reads the client contract — humans transcribe it into rate cards by hand.

Key tension to name: dead end №1 is flexible but untrustworthy; dead end №2 is trustworthy but rigid and brutally expensive. The compiler is how you get both halves.

The one-sentence answer

Compile, don't improvise.

The LLM does its expensive interpretive work once, when a rule enters the system — not on every shift. It translates the English sentence into a small, typed, deterministic check. From then on, a plain engine runs that check identically, forever. The model writes the program; it never runs the payroll.

English, once"No overtime at Mercy General this week, please."
→
Compiled, onceA typed rule: scope, condition, effect, parameters — plus generated tests.
→
Deterministic, foreverThe engine runs it on every shift. Same input, same verdict, every time.

This is not our invention — it's the pattern AWS (Automated Reasoning checks), Norm Ai, Catala, Stripe Radar and Ramp all converged on independently. We researched every serious attempt; they all landed here.

"Compiler" scares people — demystify it: a compiler is just a translator from a language humans write to a language a machine executes. Ours translates payroll English into checks. The trust comes from when the LLM runs: once, at the door, where its output can be reviewed and tested — not live on every paycheck.

Word 2 of 4

The vocabulary — the only words the compiler may use.

About thirty named, typed facts the engine knows how to compute about any shift from punches, GPS, badges, schedules and rosters: worked_min · meal_start_offset_min · geofence exit · rest gap · weekly totals · implied travel speed…

  • It's the hallucination firewall. A rule that can't be expressed in these words is refused, not guessed at. The compiler cannot invent a fact the engine can't compute.
  • It's the accuracy lever. Every system we studied says the same thing: the quality of the typed vocabulary and its descriptions matters more than the model. AWS calls variable descriptions "the single most important factor."
  • It's ours. Location intelligence gives us features nobody else has — geofence dwell, badge-versus-clock gaps, impossible-travel speed. The vocabulary is where our data moat lives.
worked_minPayable worked minutes — merged, resolved, meal-adjusted
meal_start_offset_minShift start → meal start, minutes
geo_out_gap_minClock-out vs geofence-exit gap, minutes
travel_speed_mphImplied speed from previous site to this clock-in

Not in the vocabulary: "pay people for a fair number of hours" — the compiler refuses and asks, instead of guessing what "fair" computes to.

Analogy that lands: the compiler plays Scrabble with a fixed set of tiles. It can arrange them into any rule, but it cannot draw a tile that doesn't exist. That constraint is why the output is trustworthy.

Word 3 of 4

The compiler — from a sentence to a tested rule.

  • Read the sentence and ground every noun into the vocabulary — or refuse.
  • Emit a typed rule: scope (where it applies) · condition (when it fires) · effect (what it does) · parameters (the numbers you can change).
  • Surface every ambiguity as a question. "No shift longer than 12 hours" — does that include the unpaid meal break? The compiler asks; the answer becomes an explicit parameter. Never a silent interpretation.
  • Generate tests from the rule itself, and backtest it against real history: "this would have flagged 34 of last month's 1,412 shifts."
  • Activate in shadow. New rules watch for a week without touching money — Draft → Shadow → Live, the same release discipline Stripe and the fraud-ops world use.
Ops email"No shift longer than 12 hours at Bayview, and anything over 10 needs the lead's okay."

The compiler asks — does the 12-hour cap include the unpaid meal break, or worked hours only? The answer becomes a parameter.

Word 4 of 4

The engine — dumb on purpose.

The engine evaluates every shift against every in-scope rule and writes down everything it did. No model, no temperature, no surprises — milliseconds per shift, identical output for identical input. The prototype's engine runs entirely in your browser.

  • TS-COMPLETEtimesheet.out = null → discrepancy openedFlag
  • TS-COMPLETEEvidence read: geofence exit 17:01, continuous presence → proposes out = 17:01Review
  • TS-COMPLETEDana K. · supervisor: "Confirmed — she left at 5." → clock-out set to 17:01Human
  • CA-MB-01Meal 34 min, started 3.20h inPass

The LLM survives in exactly one runtime place: reading messy evidence — a missing clock-out, a supervisor's text, a crowd-exit pattern. Those rows are always labeled Review, always amber, and always end with a human. The model proposes; it never pays.

"Dumb on purpose" is the phrase to keep. All the intelligence got spent at compile time, where it's reviewable. What runs on money is arithmetic. This split — deterministic structure, LLM only at the leaves over unstructured evidence — is exactly Norm Ai's architecture for regulatory compliance.

The malleability story

Change the 5 to a 3. The program follows.

Because every rule is typed, its numbers are parameters — and parameters render as controls automatically. Editing one re-runs the whole week instantly and shows the backtest before anything goes live.

  • Consequences first. "This change would have flagged 3 of 214 Mercy shifts last week" — you see it before you commit.

Where this goes

Point the same compiler at the public rulebook.

US wage-and-hour law is a surprisingly finite corpus — roughly 5,000–10,000 documents, ~85–90% public domain, with federal regulations available as one versioned XML file. Compile it once, maintain it centrally, and every customer starts with a rulebook that provably knows every jurisdiction on day one — then their contracts and facility policies compile in as an overlay.

BASE PACKFederal + 50 states + locals — compiled and maintained by us, effective-dated. Ships with the product; zero customer work.
VERTICAL PACKHealthcare, events, warehouse quirks — learned once, shared across customers.
CUSTOMER OVERLAYTheir MSAs, facility policies and CBAs — compiled at onboarding from their documents.
THIS WEEKOne-sentence rules from chat that expire on their own.

The proof it works is Australia: Tanda and KeyPay won that market with 30–100+ pre-built, vendor-maintained award packs. The US equivalent doesn't exist. The moat is maintenance — ~49 localities changed minimum wage on one January 1st; the freshness pipeline is the subscription, and precisely what an in-house LLM experiment will never do.

Answer the "won't they just run ChatGPT" objection here: what backs the demo sentence is a curated, effective-dated corpus, tested rule packs, and per-shift receipts. None of that comes out of a chat window — same reason Neuron7 isn't "ChatGPT with manuals."
