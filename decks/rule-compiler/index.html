The rule compiler

Say a payroll rule once, in plain English. Trust it forever. This is what we built, and why.

Where we start

Paying shift workers is detective work.

Every shift has competing stories: the client's clock says one thing, our app says another, GPS says a third. Someone has to compare them, apply the rules, chase the person who knows — and only then pay. By hand, that's 15–30 minutes per disputed shift.

Same loop in every interview — manufacturing, home care, nursing homes, construction: two time records, a reconciliation step, an escalation ladder, then payroll and the invoice. The rules operators apply are real and nameable: "zero hours on an active day, 30+ hours of OT, a missed punch."

What we want

A customer should be able to say this — and never think about it again.

Sam T. · Payroll ops · Slack, Mon 9:12am"No overtime at Mercy General this week please."

That message has to become something that behaves the same way every single time, gets tested before it touches a paycheck, and can show anyone exactly why it fired. Payroll is money — "usually right" is not a product.

This sentence is the whole project. Everyone agrees AI can read rules; the hard part is that a wrong answer here is a wage claim.

Why we didn't do the obvious thing

The two obvious ways both fail.

Ask an AI, every time

Hand the rules and the shift to a chatbot. It gives a different answer on different days, there's no way to test it, and it can't show its work.

Buy the settings screens

The old enterprise way: hundreds of configuration screens, set up by consultants. It does give the same answer every time — after months of setup, at consulting prices, frozen until the next consultant visit.

$300K–$750K · 3–18 months · Every change is a ticket

One is flexible but can't be trusted. The other is trusted but can't move. We want both halves.

Receipts if pressed: Stanford found even Westlaw's and Lexis's professional legal AIs fabricate on 17–33% of queries. UKG implementations really do run $300–750K over 3–18 months, assembled from "work rule building blocks." Nothing in the US ships with rules pre-built.

What we do instead

Translate the rule once. Run it forever.

The AI reads the sentence one time, when the rule enters the system, and translates it into a simple check — like a spreadsheet formula. After that, no AI: the same check runs on every shift, the same way, every time. That's the whole trick, and it's why we call it a compiler.

English · once"No overtime at Mercy General this week, please."
→
A check · onceHold anything over 40 h at Mercy General until Sun Aug 30.
→
Every shift · foreverSame input, same answer. No AI in the loop.

And the check can only use facts we actually measure — punches, GPS, badges, schedules. If a rule needs something we can't measure, the system says so instead of guessing.

Demystify "compiler": just a translator from a language people write to a language a machine runs. The trust comes from when the AI runs — once, at the door, where its output can be reviewed and tested — never live on a paycheck. Prior art if asked: AWS Automated Reasoning checks, Norm Ai, Stripe Radar, Ramp — all converged on exactly this shape.

Every edit shows its consequences against real history first, and new or edited rules spend a week in watch-only mode before they can touch money — the same release discipline banks use for fraud rules. Laws and contracts translate the same way this Slack message did: same format, same knobs, plus a pointer to the exact clause.

The honest part

When something's unclear, it asks. When evidence is messy, a person decides.

While translating — "no shift longer than 12 hours" — does that include the unpaid lunch break? Your answer becomes one of the knobs.

  • #4821Clock-out is missing → flaggedFlag
  • #4821GPS shows her on site until 17:01 → suggests that as the clock-outReview
  • #4821Dana K. · supervisor: "Confirmed — she left at 5." → paidHuman

The AI never resolves a doubt silently and never pays anyone. It asks, it suggests, it labels its suggestions — and a person has the last word.

Two honest limits, by design: ambiguity in a rule becomes a question at translation time; messy real-world evidence gets an AI suggestion clearly marked amber, routed to a human. This split — automatic checks for structure, judgment only at the edges — is the same architecture the serious compliance players use.

Where this goes

Pre-load the law. Customers start at zero work.

US wage-and-hour law is finite — a few thousand documents, mostly public. We translate it once, keep it current, and every customer starts with the rules of every state already loaded. Then their own contracts and policies translate in on top, the same way.

ALREADY LOADEDFederal + state + city rules — we maintain them, with receipts.
THEIR DOCUMENTSContracts and site policies — translated at onboarding.
THIS WEEKOne-sentence rules from chat that expire on their own.

Day one it already knows California meal breaks, Chicago scheduling premiums, and that rounding is illegal in CA. Provably — every rule opens to its source.

Proof this wins markets: Australia — Tanda and KeyPay ship pre-built, vendor-maintained pay-rule packs and dominate there; no US equivalent exists. The maintenance is the moat and the subscription: ~49 localities changed minimum wage on a single January 1st.
